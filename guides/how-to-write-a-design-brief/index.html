How to Write a Design Brief That Gets Good Work (Template + 3 Examples)

The gap between "I paid $25 and got garbage" and "I paid $25 and got gold" is usually one document: the brief. Here is the 7-part template we use, three filled examples, and the style-reference trick that professional buyers always include.

The 7 parts: ① one-sentence objective ② audience ③ 3 likes + 3 dislikes WITH links ④ exact deliverables + file formats ⑤ rights + source files ⑥ deadline ⑦ budget honesty. A brief with all seven cuts revisions roughly in half — revisions are where cheap gigs get expensive.

The template

PartFill withExample line
1. ObjectiveOne sentence, business goal"Logo for a specialty coffee roastery opening in March"
2. AudienceWho buys, and their taste"Urban 25–40, pays for craft, hates corporate gloss"
3. References3 likes + 3 dislikes, linked"Like: Onyx Coffee Lab. Dislike: stock swoosh logos"
4. DeliverablesExact files, sizes, formats"Logo in AI/SVG + PNG transparent, 3000px"
5. RightsSource files + usage"Full transfer, editable source included"
6. DeadlineReal date + timezone"Needed by Nov 3 for print"
7. BudgetHonest range"$80–$130 for the right tier"

The style-reference trick

Part 3 does more work than everything else combined. Designers are visual professionals — three liked examples plus three disliked ones communicates taste faster than 500 adjectives. "Modern but warm" means nothing; a link to Onyx Coffee Lab plus "not this stock-swoosh thing" is a complete creative direction. Spend twenty minutes on this section and the first draft lands closer.

What NOT to put in a brief

Do not prescribe execution ("make the logo blue with a mountain") — that is doing the designer's job badly. Do not hide budget ("quote me") — honest ranges get honest tier-matched quotes. And do not set fantasy deadlines; rushed work is worse work at every price.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a design brief be?

Under one page. Seven parts, one line each: objective, audience, references (3 likes + 3 dislikes with links), deliverables and formats, rights, deadline, budget. Longer briefs get skimmed; short ones get read.

Why do designers ask for examples I like?

Because visual references communicate taste faster than words. Three liked links plus three disliked links is the single highest-leverage part of a brief — it replaces paragraphs of adjectives with a precise direction.

Should I mention my budget in the brief?

Yes. An honest range lets sellers quote the right tier instead of guessing high or lowballing and cutting corners. Budget-hiding produces more bad-fit quotes than it saves.
