How these dates are worked out

A meteor shower happens when the Earth ploughs through a trail of debris left by a comet or, occasionally, an asteroid. The bits burn up in the upper atmosphere, and because they are all travelling in parallel they appear to radiate from a single point in the sky — which is why each shower is named after the constellation it seems to come from. The peak is the night the Earth passes through the densest part of the trail, and it can be sharp or broad depending on how old and spread out that trail is. Moonlight is the other variable: a bright Moon washes out everything except the brightest meteors.

FAQ

What time of night is best?

After midnight, and best of all in the hour or two before dawn. Before midnight you are on the trailing side of the Earth and see fewer meteors.

Do I need a telescope?

No, and it would get in the way. Meteors are best seen with the naked eye from a dark site with as wide a view of the sky as you can find.

How many will I actually see?

The quoted zenithal hourly rate is a theoretical maximum under a perfectly dark sky with the radiant overhead. Realistic counts are usually well below it.

Does the Moon ruin it?

It can. A full Moon near the radiant cuts the visible count substantially, which is why the same shower is excellent one year and disappointing the next.
