How these dates are worked out

Eclipses come in seasons. Roughly every six months the new or full moon passes close enough to a lunar node for the three bodies to line up, and an eclipse happens somewhere on Earth. A solar eclipse is the Moon's shadow touching the Earth's surface — which is why it is only visible from a narrow track a couple of hundred kilometres wide, and why totality at any one spot lasts minutes. A lunar eclipse is the Earth's shadow falling on the Moon, visible from the whole night side at once and lasting over an hour. Eclipses repeat in families called saros series, each one shifted a third of the way around the globe.

FAQ

What is the difference between a total and an annular eclipse?

In a total eclipse the Moon covers the Sun completely. In an annular one the Moon is near its farthest point and appears slightly too small, leaving a ring of Sun visible.

Can I see this one where I live?

Only if you are inside the visibility region, which for a solar eclipse is a narrow track. Each event page states where it is visible.

Is it safe to look at a solar eclipse?

Not without proper solar filters. Looking at the partially covered Sun is as damaging as looking at the full Sun, and the damage is painless, so you will not notice it happening.

Why are lunar eclipses more common to see?

A lunar eclipse is visible from anywhere on the night side of the Earth at once, so roughly half the planet can watch it. A solar eclipse is visible only from a narrow track.
