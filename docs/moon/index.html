How these dates are worked out

A full moon is the moment the Moon sits opposite the Sun in ecliptic longitude, not a whole night. That is why the date can differ by a day depending on where you are, and why the Moon can look full on two consecutive evenings. The gap between one full moon and the next is the synodic month: 29 days, 12 hours and 44 minutes on average. Because that is not a whole number of days, full moon dates drift about eleven days earlier each year until a thirteenth full moon resets the sequence — the blue moon.

FAQ

What exactly is a full moon?

The instant the Moon and Sun are opposite each other in ecliptic longitude. It lasts a moment, though the Moon looks full for roughly a day either side.

Why does the date differ between sources?

Because the moment is converted into different time zones. A full moon at 23:40 UTC is already the next day in Sydney and still the previous evening in Los Angeles.

What is a blue moon?

Two full moons in one calendar month. It happens roughly every two to three years because the synodic month is shorter than most calendar months.

How accurate are these times?

They are computed from lunar theory and are accurate to within a minute or so, which is far finer than anything you could observe by eye.
