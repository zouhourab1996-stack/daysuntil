How these dates are worked out

An equinox or solstice is a single moment, not a day. The March and September equinoxes are when the Sun crosses the celestial equator and day and night come closest to equal; the June and December solstices are when the Earth's axial tilt is at its most extreme toward or away from the Sun, giving the longest and shortest days. Because the Earth's orbit is elliptical and the calendar year is not a whole number of days, the exact minute shifts from year to year, and the date can move by a day across a leap-year cycle.

FAQ

Is the solstice a whole day?

No — it is the exact moment the tilt is at its maximum. We call the whole day the solstice because that is how calendars work, but the astronomical event lasts an instant.

Why is the longest day not the hottest?

Because the oceans and land take weeks to warm up. The seasonal lag means peak temperatures arrive a month or more after peak sunlight.

Why does the date move?

The tropical year is about 365.2422 days, not 365. The leap-year system absorbs the difference, which makes the exact moment drift and then snap back.

Are the equinoxes really equal day and night?

Very nearly, but not exactly: refraction lifts the Sun slightly and the day is measured from the edge of the disc rather than its centre.
