How these dates are worked out

Theatrical dates work like game releases: they are commercial announcements and they move. A date is firm once the studio has it on a dated trailer or in exhibition materials, and soft before that. Studios also shift dates to avoid colliding with a competing tentpole, which is why a release can move twice in a year without anything having gone wrong.

How we decide a theatrical date is real

We take a date from the studio or the distributor, not from a listing aggregator. A dated trailer, a press release or exhibition material counts; a cinema chain page does not, because those are provisional until the studio confirms. Studios also move dates to avoid colliding with a competing release, which is why a film can shift twice in a year without anything having gone wrong in production.

Territory is the part people miss. A "global release" is the exception rather than the rule, and most films open in some markets a week or two before or after the domestic date. If you are counting down outside the film's home market, check the local date before trusting the countdown.

FAQ

How reliable are these dates?

Reasonably firm inside six months, much less so beyond a year. We mark the source on each page.

Do release dates differ by country?

Often. A global release is the exception rather than the rule, and territories frequently open a week or two apart.

Why do studios move dates?

Mostly to avoid competition, sometimes because post-production ran long. Neither is unusual.

Will you update if it slips?

Yes. The countdown updates to the corrected date when the studio confirms one.

Do release dates differ by country?

Frequently. A simultaneous global release is the exception rather than the rule, and territories often open a week or two apart.

How far ahead are film dates settled?

Studios announce dates years out and then move them to avoid competition. A date inside six months is reasonably firm; anything beyond a year is a placeholder.
