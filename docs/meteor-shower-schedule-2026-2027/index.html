Meteor Shower Schedule 2026–2027: Peak Dates & Viewing Guide

The next major peak is the Geminids on December 13–14, 2026. This calendar covers the main showers through October 2027, including peak nights, active dates, Moon conditions and the best way to watch.

Peak dates are predictions, not guarantees. Local visibility depends on your time zone, latitude, weather, light pollution and the height of the radiant.

The next three peaks to plan for

Geminids — December 13–14, 2026

The Geminids are normally the year's most productive major shower. The IMO lists an ideal-sky ZHR of about 150 and a thin crescent Moon around 15% illuminated for the 2026 peak, making the lunar conditions favorable. Unlike many showers, Geminid activity can be useful before midnight once Gemini is well placed, around 22:00 local time. The radiant rises earlier than for many other showers, so observers do not have to wait until the small hours to begin.

For the live countdown and the event-specific date, see Geminids 2026.

Ursids — December 21–22, 2026

The Ursids are a smaller northern-hemisphere shower, usually producing around 5–10 meteors per hour under favorable conditions. The 2026 peak coincides with a bright waxing gibbous Moon near 89% illumination. That does not cancel the shower, but it will make faint meteors difficult to see. If you try, choose a location with a clear northern sky and observe when the Moon is below the horizon if the timing allows. See Ursids 2026.

Quadrantids — January 3–4, 2027

The Quadrantids can be very strong, but their sharp maximum lasts only a few hours. The IMO predicts the 2027 peak near 03:30 UT on January 4. A thin waning crescent Moon, about 18% illuminated, should leave the sky comparatively dark. The radiant is far north, so the shower favors northern latitudes; observers in the southern hemisphere see fewer members. Our Quadrantids 2027 countdown tracks the date.

How to watch a meteor shower properly

  1. Get away from artificial light. A dark rural site usually makes a bigger difference than buying equipment. Avoid direct streetlights and bright screens.
  2. Give your eyes time. Allow roughly 20–30 minutes for dark adaptation. Keep your phone dim or put it away.
  3. Use your naked eyes. A telescope or binoculars show too little sky at once. Lie back or use a reclining chair and watch a broad area.
  4. Do not stare only at the radiant. Meteors can appear far from the constellation they seem to originate from; looking somewhat away from the radiant can show longer trails.
  5. Check the Moon and weather. A clear, moonless sky is ideal. If the Moon is bright, try after moonset or keep it behind you.
  6. Be patient and dress for the night. Activity comes in bursts. Bring warm layers, water and a comfortable seat, and give yourself at least an hour if conditions are good.

What the meteor rate actually means

Calendar figures often quote the zenithal hourly rate (ZHR). It is a standardized estimate of the number of shower meteors an experienced observer might see in an hour under ideal dark-sky conditions, with the radiant at the zenith. It is not a guaranteed local count. A low radiant, clouds, city glow, a bright Moon or an inexperienced observer can reduce the number you actually see substantially.

That is why a ZHR of 150 for the Geminids does not mean every backyard observer will see 150 meteors per hour. Treat it as a way to compare showers, not a promise.

Which 2027 showers are best from each hemisphere?

Northern Hemisphere: the Quadrantids and Perseids are particularly well placed, while the Geminids and Ursids are also useful northern-sky targets. The Quadrantids' short peak makes timing important.

Southern Hemisphere: the Eta Aquariids and Southern Delta Aquariids are strong choices. The Eta Aquariids are especially favorable from southern tropical latitudes; north of the equator their radiant is lower and the best window is usually before dawn.

Both hemispheres: the Geminids can be seen from both, though southern observers generally see fewer. The Alpha Capricornids are also visible on either side of the equator and are worth watching for occasional bright fireballs.

Why Moonlight matters in this calendar

The 2027 Lyrid maximum occurs around a full Moon, and the Perseids and Orionids also face substantial lunar interference. You can still see the brighter meteors, but do not expect the same faint-star background or meteor counts as a dark-sky year. In contrast, the Eta Aquariids, Southern Delta Aquariids and Quadrantids have favorable lunar conditions in this calendar.

Moonrise and moonset depend on your location. Check the local Moon times for your observing site rather than relying only on a global peak date.

Frequently asked questions

What is the next major meteor shower in 2026?

The Geminids are the next major shower after November 2026. Their predicted peak is the night of December 13–14, 2026, and the International Meteor Organization lists favorable Moon conditions with about 15% illumination.

What is the strongest meteor shower in 2026?

The Geminids are usually the strongest annual shower, with an ideal-sky zenithal hourly rate near 150 in the 2026–2027 calendar. That is a standardized rate under dark, ideal conditions, not a promise that every observer will see 150 meteors in an hour.

When do the Quadrantids peak in 2027?

The Quadrantids peak on January 3–4, 2027. The IMO calendar predicts a maximum near 03:30 UT on January 4, with a thin waning crescent Moon.

Can I see meteor showers without a telescope?

Yes. Meteor showers are best watched with the naked eye because meteors can appear across a wide area of sky. Choose a dark location, let your eyes adapt, and avoid looking only at the radiant.

Does a full Moon ruin a meteor shower?

Moonlight makes faint meteors harder to see, but it does not stop the shower. During a bright Moon, observe after moonset if possible, keep the Moon behind you, and focus on the brightest meteors.
